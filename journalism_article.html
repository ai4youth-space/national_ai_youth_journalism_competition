When a Machine Wrote Bach: Did It Really Pass the Test?

GPT-6 Astra aced a fixed test for Bach-style counterpoint. What a composer heard missing — and what I found when I tried making music with the same AI — is a different story than the score suggests.

By Jacqueline Liu For 2026 National Youth AI Journalism Competition

461,400 people watched an AI ace a test designed to catch musical mistakes. Auggie's chorale had no voice-leading errors. But according to the composer I later interviewed, it also had very little of what makes Bach sound like Bach: the suspensions, tensions, and delayed resolutions that give the music its sense of breath.

That gap interested me more than the perfect score.

So I turned to GPT-6 Astra and Suno with a different question: If AI can follow the rules, can a human still teach it to make us feel something?

A Chorale Goes Viral

On September 3, 2026, OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra, presenting it as a major advance in computer use, software engineering, professional work, science, and visually demanding creative tasks. Its launch materials also included evaluations involving design and string-quartet notation, alongside demonstrations in areas such as game development and 3D environments.

Two days later, Astra became the subject of a much narrower and more provocative experiment in the music world.

A developer and musician who goes by Auggie, author of the Augmented Fifth Substack, tested Astra on what he calls the Bach Benchmark: a recurring prompt he uses to compare language models on traditional four-part writing.

The task was simple to state but technically demanding: write a four-part chorale in the style of J.S. Bach, in G minor and 3/4 time, using LilyPond notation, with soprano and alto on the upper staff and tenor and bass below.

On September 5, Auggie posted Astra's result on X, calling it the strongest performance he had seen on the Bach Benchmark to date. The post quoted his own July test of Claude Fable 5, explicitly framing Astra's result as a comparison with the previous benchmark attempt.

Auggie later published a fuller analysis on Augmented Fifth. By his assessment, Astra's chorale contained no voice-leading errors, the first time that had happened in the history of his benchmark. He also highlighted its use of passing tones, which he said no previous model had introduced in the test, and a Neapolitan sixth chord, evidence of a broader harmonic vocabulary than earlier attempts.

The example quickly became a simpler headline: an AI had written an unusually competent Bach-style chorale.

At roughly the same time, other users were demonstrating Astra in very different musical workflows. One public demo used the model to build an interactive virtual piano centered on Bach's Brandenburg Concertos. Another, from developer Pietro Schirano, connected Astra to Ableton Live through MCP tooling and showed it creating instruments, musical parts, and an arrangement inside the production environment.

Why People Are Calling It a Big Deal

The remarkable part is not that a computer can write a Bach chorale. Computers have been doing that, in one form or another, for decades.

The remarkable part is that Astra is not a Bach machine.

Earlier systems built for chorale harmonization were specialists. They were designed around music-theory rules, harmonic constraints, or datasets tailored to a very particular task. Astra, by contrast, is a general-purpose model being asked, in ordinary language, to juggle harmony, counterpoint, meter, notation, spacing, and style all at once, then output a valid score.

On Auggie's benchmark, earlier general-purpose models kept tripping over the details: parallel fifths, parallel octaves, awkward spacing, or other voice-leading violations. Astra, according to his grading, produced the first result with no voice-leading errors at all.

That matters because four-part writing is a coordination problem. Each individual note may be plausible, yet the piece can still fail if the voices move incorrectly in relation to one another. The model has to keep several musical rules active simultaneously across time, not simply predict the next likely chord.

And Astra appeared to do more than stay inside the lines.

Its use of a Neapolitan sixth and passing tones stood out because neither was explicitly requested. Those are the kinds of details that make the result more interesting than a mechanical rule-following exercise.

The model was not merely avoiding mistakes; it was introducing stylistic devices that fit the musical context. Auggie says the passing tones were a first for any model he had tested on the benchmark.

That still does not mean Astra "understands Bach," and technical correctness is not the same thing as artistic quality. A grammatically perfect sentence can still say nothing interesting, and music can have the same problem. But the result suggests that general-purpose models are getting better at something more difficult than imitation: holding a web of specialized constraints together while making choices that were not explicitly specified in the prompt.

That is the more consequential capability.

The Ableton Live demo pushes the story another step further. The interesting question is no longer just, Can an AI generate a piece of music?

It is: Can it work inside the same creative environment as the musician?

Once a model can create instruments, MIDI parts, arrangements, and editable session material directly inside a DAW, the output stops being a finished artifact handed to the user and starts becoming raw material for collaboration. A musician can change it, reject it, rearrange it, ask for another version, or continue building on top of it.

That is a very different relationship from typing a prompt and receiving an MP3.

Not Everyone Heard Bach

The reception was not unanimously positive, and the pushback is arguably the more interesting story.

Skeptics pointed out that rule-based and statistical Bach chorale generation is a well-known AI benchmark going back to systems in the 1990s. In this view, GPT-6 Astra is doing the same task better, not doing something new in kind.

Other commentators argued that Bach's four-part chorales were devotional works written for Lutheran liturgical use, and that judging success purely on voice-leading rules strips out the theological and expressive purpose the form existed to serve.

Composer, pianist, programmer, and music-technology developer Dr. Liang Liang, who reviewed the benchmark examples, argues that avoiding mistakes is not the same as writing good music.

He noted that the pieces make relatively limited use of the devices that help give Bach's writing its character: passing tones, suspensions, appoggiaturas, pedal points, and richer patterns of harmonic tension and release. An unusual chord such as a Neapolitan sixth may demonstrate technical range, he said, without showing that the model has absorbed the deeper musical language of the style.

What I Learned by Making Music With AI

The most useful result of my experiments was not proving that AI could compose music. It was discovering where AI helped most and where it still depended on me.

I first used GPT-6 Astra to compose instrumental music in ABC notation. One experiment began as a Dorian piece in 7/8 before I changed its meter and musical direction. Another was a fast C Hijaz waltz for flute, violin, and piano, inspired by a mystical scene involving a lantern, an underground chamber, and a faceless dancer.

A pattern emerged: Astra responded well after I identified a musical problem, but it did not always recognize what the piece needed on its own. The model generated possibilities; I supplied much of the listening, judgment, and direction.

My third experiment began with music I had already written. Three years ago, I composed a song's lyrics and melody but did not have the production skills to turn it into a finished recording. Instead of asking Astra to replace my composition, I used it as an arranging partner.

The division of labor was simple: I wrote the song. Astra helped shape the arrangement. Suno helped me hear it.

I preferred the result that preserved more of my original musical ideas. Giving AI more creative control did not automatically produce better music. That changed my question: instead of asking whether AI can replace a composer, I began asking how it can help a composer expand an idea that was already theirs.

The Barrier That Disappeared

Using AI did not make my role disappear. It relocated the judgment Liang Liang says benchmarks cannot measure: the choice of what to keep. I still chose the words, wrote the melody, defined the emotion, evaluated the results, and decided what needed to change.

What disappeared was part of the barrier between having an idea and being able to hear a version of it.

For young people who have melodies, lyrics, and half-formed musical ideas but have not learned production software, that change could be enormous. AI can give them a way to experiment, listen, and revise before they have mastered every technical tool.

I do not want a future in which AI replaces the effort of learning music or makes every song sound the same. I want tools that help more people begin creating while still leaving room for them to develop their own judgment, skills, and artistic voices. The future I hope for is not music without musicians; it is a future in which more people get the chance to become one.
